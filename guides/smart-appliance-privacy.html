How to stop smart appliances phoning home

Your television does not need to contact the manufacturer to display a video. But blocking the wrong domain can break casting, updates or voice control. Here is a safer order of operations.

Step 1: see what each device actually does

Before blocking anything, observe. Put a DNS resolver between the device and the network and log which domains it asks for. The pattern matters: a handful of functional endpoints plus a cluster of analytics and telemetry hosts.

Step 2: block by device, not by network

A blanket blocklist applied to every device is blunt. The same domain can be harmless for your laptop and unwanted for your TV. Per-appliance classification lets you allow functional traffic while blocking telemetry on one device only.

Step 3: allow-list function first

Start from what must keep working — firmware updates, casting, local control, authentication. Allow those explicitly, then block everything else you observed. If something breaks, you have a precise place to look.

Step 4: avoid TLS interception

Intercepting HTTPS gives you visibility but introduces a root certificate on every device, breaks certificate pinning, and creates a new attack surface. DNS-level classification gets most of the benefit without touching encrypted payloads.

Why free ad-blockers are not quite the same job: Pi-hole and AdGuard Home are excellent at network-wide ad blocking. They do not know which appliance made a request, so they cannot allow functional traffic per device.

Step 5: keep the ruleset current

Vendors change hostnames. A ruleset that is not maintained silently stops working. Check that updates are signed and that the update process is local and verifiable.

Smart Appliance Shield implements this approach: per-appliance classification, a signed 5,238-pattern ruleset, no TLS interception, and a local dashboard. It runs on Home Assistant or OpenWrt.
